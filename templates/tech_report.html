1. Introduction

The ESL program at Assumption College, Thonburi, has been designated as an English Educational Partner of the University of Cambridge's language teaching and assessment unit. As the new "CEL" centre takes shape, teachers are expected to develop a clearer understanding of their students' specific weaknesses in grammar, lexis, phonology and discourse. With this knowledge, they can tailor instruction to address those gaps more effectively.

This report seeks to create baseline knowledge about the current lexico-grammatical capabilities of students enrolled in the "A" streams at M2-M3 level. The following approach has been taken:

  1. Writing samples from formative and summative assessments were collected across the 2025–2026 and 2026–2027 academic years. Students' writing was scanned, transcribed, and stored in a Supabase database. The database contains 1,017 individual writing samples from 160 unique students across six M2-A and M3-A classes.
  2. The students' writing was analysed using Cambridge University's Error Recognition and Revision Tool (ERRANT) [https://github.com/chrisjbryant/errant]. This involves generating linguistically perfect sentences and matching them against students' sentences. From there it is possible to count and categorise the errors the students make.

The results of this analysis are presented below.

A guide to ERRANT notation

Each error is assigned a two-part code: an operation prefix and a category. The three operation prefixes are:

R — Replacement — the student used the wrong form. Example: "She run fast" → "She runs fast" (R:VERB:SVA).

M — Missing — the student left out a required word. Example: "Teacher is here" → "The teacher is here" (M:DET).

U — Unnecessary — the student added a word that should not be there. Example: "I like the dogs" → "I like dogs" (U:DET).

Research context

Hartshorne et al. (2018, pp. 1, 9), studying 669,498 English learners, found grammar-learning ability remains strong until approximately age 17.4. My M2–M3 students (aged 13–15) are still within the optimal window for grammatical development, which strengthens the case for targeted intervention at this level.

4. What error rates do B1 and B2 learners typically achieve?

To contextualise my students' error rates, I drew on learner corpus research. Error rate research is quite undeveloped and there is little conclusive evidence about error rates which are typical for ESL/EFL learners at the various CEFR levels. One study which has made an attempt to identify error rates was written as an MA thesis by Štulrajterová (2023), who analysed Czech EFL learners' writing at B1 and B2 levels using the Louvain error-tagging taxonomy, with texts rated by CEFR-trained experts. She found mean error rates of 18.92 per 100 words for B1 (p. 78) and 15.06 per 100 words for B2 (pp. 90, 103, 110). These provide empirically grounded benchmarks for what intermediate learners actually produce.

Importantly, the methodology used by Štulrajterová differs from mine: her data were manually annotated by human raters using the Louvain taxonomy, whereas my pipeline uses ERRANT for automatic error detection following LLM-based minimal-edit correction. While both approaches normalise error counts to a per-100-words basis, automatic annotation captures a narrower set of edit operations (replacements, insertions, deletions) and may undercount certain error types compared to exhaustive manual coding. This means my observed rates are not directly comparable, but the developmental trend — fewer errors at higher CEFR levels — is consistent across both methodologies.

In my own data, M2 students average {{ "%.1f"|format(m2.mean_error_rate) }}% and M3 students average {{ "%.1f"|format(m3.mean_error_rate) }}% — well above the B1 benchmark of ~19%. Only about a quarter of submissions (27.4%) fall at or below the B1 benchmark. This gap between my students' current performance and the rates observed in CEFR-aligned research underscores the need for targeted, sustained intervention.

5. How can teachers use this report?

1. Teach capitalisation as a discrete skill. Instruction on sentence-initial capitals, proper nouns, acronyms, and titles could reduce total errors by nearly 20%.

2. Build a noun-phrase unit. Pluralisation, articles, and possessives taught in sequence with explicit Thai–English comparisons make the error patterns understandable.

3. Use individual ERRANT profiles. Each student receives a personalised error breakdown, enabling differentiation by error type rather than holistic level.

4. Target verb errors. Verb-related errors total {{ "%.1f"|format(codes[3].percentage + codes[4].percentage + codes[6].percentage + codes[8].percentage) }}% of all mistakes.

The interlanguage framework (Selinker, 1972, p. 214) indicates that learner errors are systematic rather than random. Wei (2008, p. 127) distinguishes error reappearance, which is normal in low-proficiency learners, from competence fossilisation, which represents a more permanent plateau. My students fall largely into the former category, suggesting that targeted, consistent feedback can produce measurable improvement.

7. A student tracked over time: Elle

To understand how individual students develop over an academic year, I tracked Elle across two academic years: M2 (2026) and M3 (2027). Her error rate at each point was recorded through the ERRANT pipeline, giving a longitudinal view of her writing accuracy.

Elle's error rate began at 32% in Term 1 Midterm of M2 — well above the B1 benchmark of ~19%. By Term 1 Finals she had dropped to 20%, a notable improvement of 12 percentage points. Her error rate rose slightly to 24% at Term 2 Midterm (a common pattern as new, more complex writing tasks are introduced), then settled at 21% by Term 2 Finals. In the following academic year (M3, 2027), her diagnostic check recorded 36% — a classic example of error reappearance as she adjusted to new M3-level task demands — before dropping to 18% on a subsequent formative writing exercise.

This trajectory is consistent with the interlanguage framework (Selinker, 1972, p. 214): learner errors are systematic, not random, and improvement is rarely linear. The spike at Term 2 Midterm likely reflects the introduction of new task demands rather than a loss of skill — a phenomenon Wei (2008, p. 127) describes as error reappearance in low-proficiency learners, which is distinct from competence fossilisation.

Importantly, while Elle improved substantially from her baseline (32% → 21%), she remains above the B1 benchmark of ~19%. Her error rates consistently place her around the 80th percentile of her cohort, meaning roughly 80% of her peers achieve lower (i.e., better) error rates. This makes her trajectory particularly informative: even a student performing near the middle-to-upper range of the class still requires sustained intervention to reach the error rate benchmark. Her progress suggests that explicit, targeted feedback is effective, but continued support across multiple terms is needed to bring her within range of the target.

Technical Information for Teachers Who Want to Use ERRANT Analysis

8. How was the report compiled?

Writing samples from routine classroom assessments (May–July 2026) were transcribed, corrected using minimal-edit principles, then annotated with ERRANT (Bryant et al., 2017). ERRANT extracts every difference between original and corrected text using a grammar-aware alignment algorithm, then classifies each edit as Missing (M), Replacement (R), or Unnecessary (U) across 25 linguistic categories. Human experts rated 95% of automatic edits as good or acceptable (Bryant et al., 2017).

9. What is involved in an ERRANT Analysis?

ERRANT (Automatic Annotation and Evaluation of Error Types for Grammatical Error Correction; Bryant et al., 2017) is a two-stage framework. Edit extraction identifies every difference between original and corrected text using a grammar-aware alignment algorithm. Edit classification labels each edit as Missing (M), Replacement (R), or Unnecessary (U) across 25 categories: NOUN, VERB, DET, ORTH, SPELL, and others. For example, M:DET indicates a missing determiner; R:ORTH indicates a capitalisation or spacing error. The system permits analysis at any level of granularity, from broad supercategories to individual subtypes. An individual student report generated by this pipeline is shown in Appendix 2.

10. A real example

To illustrate how ERRANT analysis works in practice, consider this submission from one of my students writing about what they like most about themselves. The original text contains numerous errors across multiple categories:

Original: what I like most about me The thing that i lik my self the most is. my love for my sports example basketball Basketball help me to find lots of friends. most of my friends that i have is from basketball and my favorite part of playing basketball is the enegry that i puts in this sport.

Corrected: What I like most about myself, the thing that I like about myself the most, is my love for my sports, for example basketball. Basketball helps me to find lots of friends. Most of my friends that I have are from basketball, and my favorite part of playing basketball is the energy that I put into this sport.

The corrected version was generated by DeepSeek Flash V4 set to a temperature of 0.1, with instructions to make minimal edits (correct errors without rewriting). This follows recent trends in grammatical error correction (GEC) research, where large language models have increasingly been used for error correction alongside traditional rule-based and sequence-to-sequence systems (Bryant et al., 2023, pp. 663–665). The minimal-edit approach ensures that only genuine errors are changed, preserving the student's voice and intended meaning.

The raw ERRANT output annotates each error directly in the text, marking every deviation between what the student wrote and the corrected version:

Sentence 1
what [R:ORTH] I like most about me [OTHER] The thing that i [R:ORTH] lik [R:SPELL] my self [R:ORTH] the most is. [R:VERB:TENSE]

Sentence 2
my love for my sports [R:NOUN] example basketball Basketball help [R:VERB:SVA] me to find lots of friends.

Sentence 3
most [R:ORTH] of my friends that i [R:ORTH] have is [R:VERB:SVA] from basketball and my favorite part of playing basketball is the enegry [R:SPELL] that i [R:ORTH] puts [R:VERB:TENSE] in [R:PART] this sport.

Each error is flagged at its exact position with an error code in [bold brackets]. For example, what at the start of sentence 1 is marked [R:ORTH] because the first letter should be capitalised (orthographic error). A single error code often applies to multiple occurrences — [R:ORTH] appears 9 times across the paragraph, making it by far the most frequent error type for this student.

ERRANT detected and classified 21 errors in this short paragraph. The table below breaks down the error types:

Several observations stand out. The student knows the content they want to express — the corrected version preserves the original meaning almost exactly. The errors cluster in surface-level mechanics (capitalisation, spelling) and grammatical form (verb agreement, prepositions), rather than in content or organisation. This pattern is typical of A2–B1 learners: strong communicative intent undermined by insufficient accuracy in basic conventions.

Importantly, the error rate for this student is 37% — well above the B1 benchmark of ~19%. However, the category-level breakdown reveals a focused intervention path: targeted work on capitalisation alone (9 of 21 errors) would reduce the error rate by nearly half.

11. What are the limitations of ERRANT Analysis?

  • Correction accuracy. Annotation quality depends on the accuracy of the correction step. Minimal-edit prompting was used, but some overcorrection remains possible.
  • Coverage gaps. Eight observed error codes fell outside the standard ERRANT mapping; 2.7% of errors were classified as OTHER.
  • Cross-sectional design. This is a single time-point analysis. Cohort differences may reflect group variation rather than developmental progress.
  • Low word count. The mean word count is {{ "%.0f"|format(o.mean_word_count) }} tokens. Error rates derived from very short texts are less stable.
  • Task variability. Writing tasks were not standardised (description, opinion, narrative). Task-related effects were not controlled in the analysis.
